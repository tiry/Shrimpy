# Cyanobacteria

Background only. `references/triage.md` owns the response.

## What it is, and why the name matters

Cyanobacteria are autotrophic gram-negative **bacteria** that photosynthesise. They are not
algae, despite "blue-green algae" being the name everyone uses, and the distinction is the
single most useful fact on this page:

- **Algae treatments do not work on it**, because it is not an alga.
- **Algae-eating animals largely will not touch it.** Nerites, otocinclus and shrimp graze
  around it. Expecting the cleanup crew to handle it wastes time while it spreads.

In the tank it appears as a dark blue-green, sometimes blackish or purplish, slimy sheet
over substrate, glass and plant leaves. It lifts off in sheets rather than in strands — the
texture is the giveaway — and it has a distinctive earthy, musty smell that is often noticed
before the mat is.

## Why it appears

The usual combination is poor circulation plus surplus nutrients plus light. Dead spots
where water barely moves are where mats start, which is why it so often begins in a corner
or behind an ornament while the rest of the tank looks fine.

One property makes it unlike any algae problem: **many cyanobacteria fix atmospheric
nitrogen**, converting dissolved N₂ into a usable form. So starving the tank of nitrate — the
standard answer to an algae bloom — does not starve cyanobacteria, and can make matters
worse by weakening the plants that would otherwise compete with it. Phosphate, organic waste
and stagnant flow matter more here than nitrate does.

## Why it is not harmless

Two reasons beyond appearance:

- **It smothers.** A mat over a leaf blocks light and gas exchange and the plant beneath it
  dies, adding to the organic load that fed the mat.
- **Some strains produce toxins.** Cyanotoxins are a genuine phenomenon in freshwater
  systems, and while a tank mat is not a bloom on a lake, this is a reason not to treat a
  mat as merely unsightly, and a reason to remove rather than to tolerate.

The decay of a large mat also consumes oxygen, which is why clearing a heavy infestation all
at once can cause a second problem. `references/chemistry.md` covers the oxygen mechanism.

## What actually addresses it

Physical removal plus flow plus fixing the nutrient source. Siphon the mats out rather than
stirring them in, restore circulation to the dead spot, and find what is loading the tank —
uneaten food or something decaying, the same root cause as
`assets/wiki/pest/planaria.md`.

Note that antibiotic treatments exist and work, because it is a bacterium. They also damage
the tank's nitrifying bacteria, which is not a trade to make casually in a shrimp tank —
see `assets/wiki/product/stability.md` for what that population does and how slowly it
returns. `references/triage.md` first.
