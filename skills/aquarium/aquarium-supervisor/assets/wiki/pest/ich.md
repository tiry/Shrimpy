# Ichthyophthirius multifiliis

Background only. `references/triage.md` owns the response, and the response in a shrimp
tank is not the response in a fish tank.

## What it is

A parasitic ciliate, and the only species in its genus — the cause of white spot disease,
one of the most common and most lethal diseases of freshwater fish. It affects fish only.
**Shrimp and snails cannot catch it.**

The visible sign is what names it: small white spots, like grains of salt, scattered over
the body and fins. Before the spots appear the fish often flicks and rubs against surfaces,
which is the earlier and more useful warning.

## The life cycle is the whole problem

The parasite is only vulnerable during part of its cycle, and understanding that explains
every piece of standard advice:

1. **On the fish**, burrowed under the skin or gill epithelium, feeding and growing. This is
   the stage that is visible — and **the stage nothing can reach.** The host's own tissue
   shields it, which is why a treatment that appears to do nothing for several days is not
   necessarily failing.
2. **Off the fish**, dropping to the substrate and encysting.
3. **Dividing** inside the cyst into hundreds of free-swimming infective cells.
4. **Swimming** in search of a host, and dying within a day or so if they do not find one.

Only stages three and four are exposed to treatment. So a course must run long enough to
catch every parasite through a full cycle, and stopping when the spots disappear stops at
precisely the wrong moment — the spots vanish because the parasites left the fish to divide,
not because they died.

Temperature drives the cycle speed. Warmer water shortens it, which is why raising
temperature is a standard part of the response: it forces the parasite through the
vulnerable stage faster. It also lowers dissolved oxygen, which is its own risk.

## Why this page is in a shrimp keeper's wiki

Because the disease is harmless to the invertebrates and **the cures are not**.

The standard remedies are built on malachite green, formalin, and copper. Copper is the
critical one: it is acutely toxic to shrimp at very low concentrations — see
`assets/wiki/species/neocaridina.md` — and it adsorbs onto substrate and decor, leaching
back for a long time afterwards. **A copper-treated tank is not reliably safe for shrimp
again**, which makes this a decision about the tank's future, not just this week.

So the realistic options in a mixed tank are to treat the fish elsewhere, or to use the
heat-and-salt approaches that do not involve copper, accepting that shrimp tolerate salt
poorly too. Neither is free, and choosing between them needs the actual stocking — run
`aqua livestock` before deciding anything.

Check every bottle for copper before it goes near a tank holding invertebrates, including
products that do not advertise it. `assets/wiki/product/melafix.md` covers a related case
where a product marketed as gentle is still not appropriate here.
