# Planaria

Background only. This page explains what the animal is and what treating it would cost.
**It is not a treatment procedure** — `references/triage.md` owns what to do, and any dose
comes from `aqua`, never from here.

## What it is

A planarian is a free-living flatworm of the order Tricladida, found in marine, freshwater
and terrestrial environments. The aquarium ones are freshwater Continenticola: flat,
soft-bodied, a few millimetres to a couple of centimetres, gliding rather than swimming,
usually white or pale brown. The blunt arrow-shaped head with two eyespots is the reliable
identification — it is what separates a planarian from the harmless detritus worms that
share the same glass.

They are famous for regeneration. A planarian cut in pieces regrows into several whole
animals, which is a laboratory curiosity and, in a tank, the reason **physical removal
alone does not work**. Siphoning and trapping reduce the population; they do not end it.

## Why they are in the tank

They are almost always introduced — on plants, in substrate, in the water of a bag of new
livestock. A few arrive, and nothing is visible for weeks.

What makes them visible is **surplus food**. Planaria populations track available protein,
so a visible bloom is a feeding signal before it is a pest problem: uneaten food, an
over-generous protein supplement, or a dead animal that was never found. This matters more
than it sounds. Cutting the food supply is the one intervention with no side effects, and
it addresses the cause rather than the symptom.

## Why they matter for shrimp

For fish they are close to harmless. For a shrimp colony they are a genuine threat, but a
narrow one — the danger is to what cannot get away:

- **Newly hatched shrimplets**, which at roughly 1 mm are within range
- **Eggs**, including those on a berried female that is already stressed
- **A shrimp mid-molt**, soft-shelled and immobile for a period after shedding

An adult shrimp in good condition is not prey. So the honest reading of a planaria bloom in
a breeding colony is a recruitment problem — the adults stay fine while the young stop
appearing. If a colony has quietly stopped producing juveniles, this belongs on the list of
causes.

Do not confuse them with **Scutariella japonica**, the small white commensal that sits on a
shrimp's rostrum and gills. That one rides the animal and is treated differently. A
planarian is on the glass and the substrate, not on the shrimp.

## The conflict worth knowing in advance

The standard treatments are anthelmintics. Fenbendazole, a broad-spectrum benzimidazole,
is used in freshwater shrimp tanks as a planaria and hydra treatment, and the widely sold
areca-nut ("No Planaria") products act on the same target. Both are marketed as safe for
shrimp.

**Both are hostile to snails.** Benzimidazoles and areca-nut extracts are used
deliberately to remove pest snails, and they do not distinguish a pest snail from a
deliberately kept one. In a tank stocked with mystery snails or nerites, "the shrimp-safe
treatment" is not a safe treatment — it trades a threat to the shrimplets for near-certain
loss of the snails.

That is the decision, and it is why this page exists: the treatment choice is not
planaria-versus-nothing, it is planaria-versus-the-snails, and anyone reaching for a bottle
should have been told so first. Removing the food surplus and trapping costs nothing and
conflicts with nothing; reach for it before anything else.

`references/triage.md` has the procedure. Confirm the identification, and confirm what else
is in the tank, before any product is opened.

## Hydra, briefly

Hydra turn up in the same conditions and are often mentioned in the same breath. They are
small freshwater hydrozoans — cnidarians, related to jellyfish — that anchor to a surface
and catch prey with stinging tentacles, and they regenerate as readily as planaria do. Like
planaria they bloom on surplus food and threaten shrimplets rather than adults, and they
respond to the same treatments with the same cost to snails. See
`assets/wiki/pest/hydra.md`.
