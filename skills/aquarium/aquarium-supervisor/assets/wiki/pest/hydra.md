# Hydra

Background only. `references/triage.md` owns what to do; any dose comes from `aqua`.

## What it is

A genus of small freshwater hydrozoans — cnidarians, so relatives of jellyfish and
anemones. A hydra is a thin stalk anchored to glass, plant or hardscape, topped by a ring of
tentacles, a few millimetres long and easy to miss until the population grows. Under light
they look like tiny pale threads that contract sharply when disturbed.

They are carnivores. The tentacles carry cnidocytes — stinging cells that fire a barbed
thread on contact, paralysing small prey and drawing it to the mouth. This is the same
mechanism a jellyfish uses, at a scale that matters only to very small animals.

Hydra are also famous for regeneration: cut one apart and the pieces regrow into whole
animals, which is why the genus is named after the mythical beast and why **physical removal
does not work**. They reproduce asexually by budding, so a single individual is a
population.

## Why they appear

The same reason as planaria: surplus food. Hydra populations track available prey and
nutrients, so a visible bloom is nearly always downstream of overfeeding, an uneaten protein
supplement, or something dead that was not found.

That makes the first response the same as for planaria, and it has no side effects: cut the
input, find what is decaying, and the population falls on its own. See
`assets/wiki/pest/planaria.md`.

## What is actually at risk

Adult shrimp and fish are far too large to be prey and are not harmed by contact. The threat
is narrow and real:

- **Newly hatched shrimplets**, at roughly a millimetre, are exactly the size hydra catch.
- **Fry** of any small livebearer, in the first days after release.

So, as with planaria, a hydra bloom in a breeding tank shows up as young that never appear
rather than as visible deaths. A colony where the adults are fine but recruitment has
stopped should have both on the list.

They do not harm plants, do not foul water, and do not spread to anything larger. A hydra
population in a tank with no juveniles in it is an aesthetic problem and nothing more.

## The treatment conflict

Identical to planaria's, and worth reading before reaching for a bottle: the
anthelmintics that clear hydra are the same ones that clear planaria, and **they are hostile
to snails**. Fenbendazole is used in shrimp tanks as a hydra and planaria treatment
precisely because it is safe for shrimp — it is not safe for mystery snails or nerites.

If snails are being kept deliberately, that is the decision to weigh, and it is covered in
`assets/wiki/pest/planaria.md` rather than repeated here.
