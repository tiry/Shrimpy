# Neocaridina davidi

Background only. For what this tank's shrimp need and what they are actually sitting in,
run `aqua species neocaridina` and `aqua status` — the tolerance envelope is data, not
prose, and it is not repeated here.

## What it is

A small freshwater atyid shrimp native to Taiwan, eastern China, the Korean peninsula and
Vietnam, living in lakes, ponds, rivers and streams. Adults reach roughly 3–4 cm. The wild
type is a mottled brown; every colour in the trade — red, orange, yellow, green, blue,
violet, black, white — is selective breeding of that stock, and "cherry shrimp" is the red
morph's name that came to stand for the species.

Two consequences of that breeding history matter in a tank:

- **Colour is not health.** Pigment comes from chromatophores and is influenced by
  carotenoids in the diet. A pale shrimp may be stressed, may be freshly molted, or may
  simply be a weakly-selected line.
- **Mixed morphs revert.** Cross two colour lines and the offspring trend back toward wild
  brown over generations. Keeping a colour means keeping one morph.

The species tolerates a wide range of conditions well enough to have become invasive in
thermally polluted waterways in Japan, Poland, Germany and the United States, almost
certainly through released aquarium stock. **Never release them or pour tank water into a
storm drain.**

## Diet

Omnivores that graze biofilm, algae and detritus, grabbing food with the small claws at the
ends of the first two pairs of legs. They do **not** eat healthy vascular plants — shrimp
on a dying leaf are eating the decay, not causing it, which is why "the shrimp are
destroying my plant" is nearly always a plant problem.

A mature tank feeds them more than the keeper does. Biofilm on hardscape and substrate is
the bulk of the diet, and a tank scrubbed too clean starves them quietly.

## Molting

They grow by shedding the whole exoskeleton, juveniles more often than adults. Two things
follow:

- **Leave the shed shell in the tank.** They eat it back to recover its minerals. A
  scattering of translucent white casts is a sign of a growing colony, not of deaths — a
  dead shrimp goes opaque pink-orange, a molt stays clear and hollow.
- **Molting is when they are killed by water chemistry.** Building a new cuticle needs
  dissolved calcium, and a swing in hardness or osmolarity during the molt is what turns a
  marginal parameter into a death. `references/chemistry.md` has the mechanism; the floor
  below which it fails is in `aqua species neocaridina`.

## Sexing and breeding

Sexually dimorphic and easy to read once the colony matures. Females are larger, deeper in
colour and wider in the tail; males are smaller and plainer. In a translucent morph the
developing ovaries show through the carapace behind the head as a triangular "saddle",
which means a female close to ready.

Breeding needs nothing but a sexed pair, stable water and food — there is no trigger to
engineer, and a colony that is not breeding is reporting a problem.

The sequence: the female molts, then releases pheromones that send the males swimming
agitatedly around the tank; the male deposits sperm on her body; the eggs are fertilised as
they are laid, passing out of the ovaries, and she fastens them to the swimmerets under her
tail. **Eggs are fertilised outside the body, so a berried female has certainly mated.**

A clutch is roughly 20–30 eggs, carried two to three weeks while she fans them with her
swimmerets. They darken as they develop until the eyespots of the young show through.
Maturity comes at about two months and they live one to two years.

**There is no larval stage.** The young hatch as ~1 mm copies of the adults and begin
grazing immediately. That is the single most important husbandry fact about the species: no
special feed, no separate rearing vessel, and any filter intake that can take a 1 mm animal
will.

A stressed female, or a young one carrying for the first time, will drop the clutch.
Repeated dropping is a symptom to investigate, not a quirk.

## What kills them

The failure modes worth knowing before reading the numbers, each covered properly in
`references/triage.md`:

- **Copper**, in any dose. Shrimp use haemocyanin to carry oxygen and are far more
  sensitive to it than fish. Many fish medications and some plant fertilisers contain it —
  check every bottle before it goes near the tank. See `assets/wiki/product/melafix.md` for
  a treatment that is not safe here despite being sold for community tanks.
- **A parameter swing**, more than a wrong parameter held steady. Their tolerance is broad
  and their tolerance for *change* is not.
- **Anything that kills invertebrates by design** — planaria dewormers most of all. See
  `assets/wiki/pest/planaria.md`.

Molting failure sits downstream of the first two and is usually how the damage becomes
visible.
