# Vittina waigiensis

Background only. Run `aqua species nerite` for what it needs.

## What it is

The red racer or gold racer nerite, a small freshwater snail of the family Neritidae from
the Indo-Pacific. The family is largely brackish and marine; the handful of freshwater
species in the aquarium trade are the exception, and that origin explains almost everything
distinctive about them.

They are the most effective algae grazers commonly available. Unlike most aquarium snails
they work hard films and even the tougher green spot algae that other grazers leave, and
they do it on glass, hardscape and leaves without damaging plants — nerites do not eat
healthy plant tissue.

## The breeding trap

**A nerite cannot reproduce in fresh water, but it will keep trying.**

The life cycle runs through a planktonic larval stage that requires brackish or salt water.
In a freshwater tank the adults mate and the females lay, and none of it ever comes to
anything. So the population never increases — which is the single biggest reason to choose
them, since a grazer that cannot overpopulate is rare.

The cost is the eggs. Females deposit small, hard, white capsules, individually and firmly
cemented, on glass, hardscape, equipment and especially on darker surfaces where they show.
They are inert and harmless and they never hatch, but they are genuinely difficult to
remove — they resist wiping and usually need scraping. Nothing is wrong when they appear;
they are simply the permanent cosmetic price of the animal.

This also means every nerite in the tank was collected or farmed elsewhere, and that any
losses are not replaced from within.

## Escape

Nerites climb, and they leave. They will cross the waterline, travel the rim and end up on
the floor, where they dry out and die. This is normal behaviour rather than a verdict on the
water, though a sustained exodus is worth investigating. **A tight-fitting cover is not
optional for this species** — which sits awkwardly beside the mystery snail's need for an
air gap, and both can be satisfied at once by a cover that traps humidity without sealing
the surface.

A nerite found upside down on the substrate often cannot right itself and will die there.
Turning one over costs nothing.

## Shell and health

As with any snail, the shell records the water. Erosion, pitting and a thinning outer layer
mean insufficient dissolved mineral or a pH working against deposition; see
`assets/wiki/species/mystery_snail.md` for the same reading, and `references/chemistry.md`
for the mechanism.

A nerite sealed tightly to the glass and unmoving for a day is usually fine. One that has
come away from its operculum, or that hangs loose from the shell, is not — and a dead snail
fouls water quickly for its size.

## With shrimp

Completely compatible, and a common pairing: both graze, neither predates, and the nerite
takes the algae the shrimp cannot. The treatment conflict in
`assets/wiki/pest/planaria.md` applies here exactly as it does to mystery snails.
