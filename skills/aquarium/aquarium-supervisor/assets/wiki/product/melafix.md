# API Melafix

Background on what is in the bottle. **Any dose comes from `aqua dose melafix`**, which
also records the reduced multiplier used when shrimp are present.

## What it is

An emulsion of tea tree oil — melaleuca oil, the essential oil distilled from the leaves of
*Melaleuca alternifolia*, an Australian paperbark. It is sold as a treatment for bacterial
infections, damaged fins and wounds.

Tea tree oil has genuine antimicrobial activity in vitro; that is not in dispute and it is
why the product exists. Two things complicate translating that into an aquarium.

**It is an oil, in water.** The active compounds are poorly water-soluble, which is why the
product is an emulsion and why it clouds the water. What reaches a bacterial colony on a
fish is not what a laboratory assay measured.

**The evidence for the aquarium application is weak.** Antimicrobial activity in a dish is a
long way from treating an infection on a living fish, and the clinical support for this
class of remedy is thin. It is widely used, widely reported as harmless, and much less
widely demonstrated to work. Treating it as a proven antibiotic is the error.

## The reason to be careful here specifically

Not toxicity — the recorded position is that it is tolerated by invertebrates at the label
concentration, and `aqua` holds the multiplier actually used when shrimp are present.

The problem is physical. **An oil emulsion forms a surface film**, and a film across the
water surface reduces gas exchange. In most tanks that is a minor cost. In this system it is
not, for a reason that has nothing to do with the shrimp's chemistry:

Surface agitation is doing **two** jobs here — oxygenation *and* evaporative cooling.
Anything that damps the surface degrades both at once, so the cost of the film is paid twice
and in summer it is paid when it can least be afforded. `references/chemistry.md` covers
the dissolved-oxygen mechanism, and `aqua status` will show what the tanks are actually
running at.

There is a second, well-known caution in the hobby: labyrinth fish — bettas, gouramis —
breathe atmospheric air at the surface, and a surface film is a direct problem for them
rather than an indirect one. Worth knowing even when none are kept, because it explains why
the caution exists at all.

## How to think about reaching for it

The honest summary is that it is a low-risk, low-evidence product whose main cost in this
system is the surface film rather than any toxicity. That makes it a poor first response to
a sick animal, and it makes water quality, `references/triage.md` and identifying the actual
problem the better use of the same hour.

If it is used, increased surface agitation for the duration offsets the one effect that is
genuinely predictable.
